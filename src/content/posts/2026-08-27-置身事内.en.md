# Chapter 1. Rights and Affairs of Local Government

To understand the model of government governance and operation, we must first understand the distribution rules of power and resources in the government system, including the vertical distribution of higher and lower governments, as well as the horizontal distribution of peer governments.

This chapter will introduce the basic logic of the division of intergovernmental affairs. The first section briefly introduces several features of the administrative system. In combination with the actual situation, sections 2 to 4 detail the three principles of division of power: the principle of externality and benefit scope, the principle of information complexity, and the principle of incentive compatibility. (2) Section 5 introduces the investment promotion work of local governments. The development of the economy is the core task of the government, and investment promotion requires the mobilization of various resources and means. The specific matters involved are deep and wide, far beyond the discussion of "public services" or "public goods" in mainstream economics textbooks. Understanding investment attraction is the starting point for understanding the deep integration of local governments into the economic development process.

## 1.1 Characteristics of Government Governance

The "central - county - county" three-level government management system established by the Qin Dynasty has evolved into the current five-level government management system: central - provincial - city - county - town, but in fact the situation is much more special. At present, there are ordinary prefecture-level cities and provincial-level cities (sub-provincial cities), and the actual units of prefecture-level cities are regions (cities are an easy concept to confuse, from top to bottom there are municipalities directly under the Central Government, municipalities separately listed, prefecture-level cities, county-level cities, and recently added town-level cities). The evolution of the governance system is a reflection of the needs of the times. The specific development will be troublesome and not the main content, until the point is reached. Under the three-tier administration of ancient times, "counties with no imperial power" appeared because of the constraints of administrative and financial capabilities at that time. Since the modern Republic of China, the regime has gradually extended to the streets of townships and cities below the county. However, the village level has not yet been touched, and the village is self-governed by the villagers.

### 1.1.1 Central and Local Government-Central-Local Relations

Central relations have always been the main line of research on many major issues. On the one hand, a country that maintains great unity will inevitably require the maintenance of central authority and unified leadership; on the other hand, the size of China determines that the daily operation of the political system should be dominated by local governments.

According to historian Ge Jianxiong, from 221 BC to the end of the Qing Dynasty in 1911, the time for China's "reunification" (that is, basically restoring the territory of the previous dynasty and maintaining relative peace in the Central Plains) was only 950 years, accounting for 45% of this historical period, while the time of division accounted for 55%. It can be seen that it is not easy to maintain a unified country.

Today, the importance of central relations is also reflected in the Constitution. Article 1 of the current Constitution

The second article stipulates the state and government system, and the third article immediately stipulates the general principle of central and local relations: "The division of the functions and powers of the central and local state institutions follows the principle of giving full play to local initiative and enthusiasm under the unified leadership of the central government.

### 1.1.2 Party and Government - The Division of Labour between Party and Government

Absolute leadership of the government by the Communist Party of China is the theme of political life. Simply put, the Party is responsible for major decision-making and personnel appointment and dismissal, and the government is responsible for implementation. However, the two are closely intertwined in organization and highly overlapping in personnel, so it is difficult to strictly distinguish between the two. However, there is no need to emphasize the division of party and government in the study of economic development.

### 1.1.3 Segmentation, Multiple Leadership

A distinctive feature of China's political system is "layered replication": the central government's main political structure, that is, the party committee, government, people's congress, CPPCC, etc., is completely replicated at the provincial, municipal, and county levels, that is, the so-called "four sets of teams". The main ministries and commissions of the central government, with a few exceptions such as the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, have corresponding departments at all levels of government. This vertical relationship from top to bottom is called "bar", while the horizontal government bounded by administrative divisions is called "block". Most local departments are led by both 'bars' and 'blocks'. Usually, the "bar" relationship is a business relationship, and the "block" relationship is a leadership relationship, because local party committees and governments can decide on personnel appointments and dismissals.

### 1.1.4 Superior Leadership and Coordination

When there is no clear precedent and process, clerks will “kick the ball” between departments, or simply push it to their superiors, so power and decision-making will naturally be concentrated upwards. One of the major tasks of institutional design is to avoid pushing too many decisions to superiors, reduce the burden on superiors, and improve decision-making efficiency. Therefore, one of the principles of simplifying the decision-making process in the system is to solve problems at the lowest level where consensus can be reached. If it is a departmental matter, the department leader can decide; if it is a regular cross-departmental matter, set up a superior "responsible leader" or even a "leadership group" to coordinate the advancement.

### 1.1.5 Bureaucracy

All rules and regulations must be enforced and operated by people. The same system is in the hands of different people, the effect may be completely different, and the personnel system is the core of the organization. China is the first country in the world to develop a sound, professional and complex bureaucratic system. Long before Qin unified the six countries, countries had begun to absorb talents through channels such as military merit and learning, and official positions could not be inherited, gradually weakening the aristocratic ruling system determined by blood relations. After the Tang Dynasty, the huge bureaucratic system based on imperial examinations and with a unified ideology became one of the pillars of political and social stability. The bureaucracy selected by the imperial examination is both a political leader and a moral exemplar. It is not only the core of the political system, but also a cultural and ideological carrier for maintaining the unity of the state and society. The three characteristics of this system continue to this day: officials must learn and implement a unified ideology; officials are appointed by superiors; and local governors need to rotate in many places. On the premise of maintaining great unity, these characteristics are the embodiment of the central relations balance in the personnel system.

### 1.1.6 Summary

In general, China has a political system based on its own history and culture. The actual operation of power is not completely consistent with the rules and regulations on paper, but it is by no means arbitrary. In any system, there are two constraints on the operation of power: the ability to do things and the willingness to do things. The former depends on the resources at hand, the latter on the enthusiasm and initiative of all parties.

## 1.2 Externalities and economies of scale

The scope and boundaries of local government power are determined by administrative divisions. China implements "territorial management", and local power is inseparable from administrative division, so we first analyze the division of power from the perspective of administrative division.

The primary factor affecting administrative division is “externality”, an important economic concept, which is simply that human behavior affects others. Externalities are positive and negative, positive externalities have a positive effect on others; negative externalities have a negative effect on others.

A matter should be decided by the local autonomy and can be considered from the perspective of externalities. If the matter only affects the local area and there is no externality, it should be handled by the local area; if it still affects other places, it should be coordinated by the superior. Therefore, the size of the administrative area should be consistent with the scope of the policy. If the administrative area is too small and there are too many things that need to be coordinated at a higher level, the government at this level will also lose its significance. Administrative divisions, in turn, limit the resources that local governments can allocate, limiting the reach of their policies.

### 1.2.1 Boundaries of public goods and services

Classical economics believes that the core function of government is to provide public goods and public services, because such goods are available and needed by everyone, and the more people use them, the more they can dilute costs, that is, "economies of scale". However, it is clear that public goods can only serve a limited number of people, and it is impossible to serve an infinite number of people in an infinite range, that is, public services have and only have a certain scope of influence. The division of administrative boundaries is related to the scope of influence of public services. On the one hand, because of economies of scale, the more people covered, the more cost-effective, the better the region; on the other hand, subject to the cost and willingness of people to access these services, the region cannot expand indefinitely.

The coverage of government public services is also related to technology and infrastructure, and the foundation of hardware and software is very important. Such as electricity, network, running water, satellite signals, education level, literacy rate, etc.

Using the economies of scale and borders of public goods as entry points can also help to understand some of the differences in the division of labor between central and local governments. The service covering all citizens is borne by the central government, and the service covering the local administrative district is borne by the local government. Those who need the unified standard content of the country shall be borne by the central government, and each region may be borne by the local government as stipulated by the local government.

In addition to the difference in the nature and content of public services and the level of infrastructure construction, there are other factors that affect the effectiveness of public services and thus the demarcation of borders.

### 1.2.2 Population density, geography and cultural differences

The first important factor is population density. The idea of dividing administrative areas by population density is very natural. Providing public goods and services requires a large number of people, which not only has a large tax revenue, but also can be diluted to achieve scale benefits. In densely populated places, enough people can be served on a relatively small scale to achieve scale gains, so the area of the administrative area can be smaller; where the land is sparsely populated, the administrative area should be larger.

The most important grassroots unit in China's past dynasties was the county, and the division of county areas was based on population density, which was one of the basic rules established as early as the Qin and Han Dynasties. The so-called "people's thickness is reduced, and the rarer is open" ("Hanshu · Baiguan Gongqing Table"). As the population density increases, the area of the administrative area should become smaller and the number should become larger and larger. Therefore, as the ancient economic center and population shifted from the north to the south, the administrative division gradually changed from "north to south" to "south to north".

The second important factor is geography. Ancient transportation was inconvenient, and mountains and rivers became the natural boundaries of administration, which historical geographers call "changing with mountains and rivers".

The third important factor is linguistic and cultural differences. There are differences between the dialects of Chinese, and there are also differences between Chinese and minority languages. If the language is not clear, government administration or public services may need to be differentiated, resulting in increased costs and lower scale benefits, thus affecting the division of administrative areas. Of course, linguistic differences are highly correlated with geographical differences. With the formation of dialects, communication is limited due to the blockage of mountains and rivers. Worldwide, if the distribution of terrain or arable land in a country varies greatly, the population distribution is relatively scattered, and the language variation in the country is often richer.

When you understand these factors, you can understand a lot of policies and reforms. For example, with the concentration of economic activity and population, there is a need to break down existing administrative boundaries and provide standardized public services that are seamlessly integrated on a wider scale, so there are various urban planning, some even rising to national strategies. For example, geography blocks unfavorable communication, but with infrastructure interconnection, administrative divisions can also be simplified, such as the dismantling of counties and districts. In addition, by understanding the diversity of dialects and cultures, we also understand the importance of promoting Mandarin and common cultural and historical education to maintain national unity.

But we also need to pay attention to some other considerations, such as administrative adjustments are prudent, but population density changes are very frequent and may even be very rapid. Sometimes, in order to meet the needs of political stability, it is necessary to artificially break the natural boundaries and not divide the administrative districts based on the boundaries of natural hazards, so as to prevent local forces from creating divisions based on natural hazards. This division method is called "canine tooth staggering". In addition, in reality, many dialect and cultural areas are often divided into several administrative divisions, such as Wuyuan District or Hakka Cultural District.

### 1.2.3 Economic Development of Administrative Junction Areas

There is a phenomenon in China's economy: Located at the administrative boundary (especially at the provincial boundary), economic development is generally relatively backward. The provincial boundary area accounts for one-sixth of the total land area. However, in 2012, more than half of the 592 national key counties for poverty alleviation and development were located at the provincial boundary, and the incidence of poverty was much higher than that of non-boundary counties.

This phenomenon, commonly known as the "three indifferent zones", can also be explained by the scale effect of public goods and the theory of boundaries. First of all, within a province, with the provincial capital as the political and economic center, the population is the most dense, and the economies of scale of public goods are the most significant. However, almost all provincial capitals (except Nanjing and Xining) are close to the provincial border, and this geographical distance limits the border area's access to public resources. Secondly, the delimitation of provincial boundaries is related to geographical conditions. Many provincial boundary counties are located in mountainous areas, and the slope is on average 35% higher than that of non-provincial boundary counties, which is not conducive to economic development. Third, although the demarcation of provincial boundaries is related to dialects and local cultures, it does not completely coincide. Mainstream culture within a province is generally concentrated around the provincial capital, while provincial boundary areas are often non-mainstream cultural districts in the province, and their dialects may also differ from the mainstream. These border areas are separated from the mainstream culture within the province, and the exchanges with neighboring provinces and cultural districts are divided by administrative boundaries, which is not conducive to economic development.

From the point of view of public goods, the border areas are faced first and foremost with inadequate infrastructure such as road networks. Even in 2012, the “boundary effect” in road network traffic (lower density of road networks in provincial boundary areas) still exists, although it is much better than before. Even after excluding economic development, population density, topography and other factors, the "border effect" still exists, but it is limited to the highways and provincial roads invested by the provincial government, and does not exist in the national highways and railways invested by the central government. It can be seen that the provincial government will not prioritize the allocation of limited resources to the border areas.

Another problem that has long plagued the public governance of the border is environmental pollution, especially in the major rivers, rivers, and lakes across the province, such as the Huaihe River, the Yellow River, and the Taihu Lake. This is a typical cross-regional externality issue.

Cross-regional externalities can be coordinated through common superiors across regions, which is why administrative regions should not only be divided horizontally, but also vertically. If there are interactions between subordinates that are difficult to decide on their own, they should resort to the decisions of their superiors. Conversely, the powers of all levels of government are delegated by superiors, and which powers are decentralized is also related to externalities. Subordinates generally have more decision-making power in matters that are less external. Although in principle, superiors can intervene in all affairs of subordinates, in reality, whether to intervene, to what extent to intervene, and whether to achieve the intervention effect are all subject to the externalities of public affairs, economies of scale, and the difficulty of cross-regional coordination.

Administrative boundaries affect economic development. Local protectionism and market segmentation still exist today. Especially in the factor of production market, land use indicators and household registration system have a great impact on land and population movements. In the long run, the elimination of this phenomenon requires more in-depth market-oriented reforms. However, in the short and medium term, adjusting administrative divisions, expanding the size of cities, and even building urban circles can also play a role. The current administrative divisions have inherited from the ancient society and the planned economy, and are not fully adapted to the rapid development and agglomeration of industry and modern service industries. Moreover, in a large country with great regional differences such as China, it is inevitable that the construction of a national unified large market for products and elements will be a long-term process, and it is inevitable that regional integration will be adopted first.

The basic unit of regional integration is the city, but within the city, it is first necessary to integrate urban and rural areas. Under the city-managed county system, with the development of urbanization, the demand for public services will become more and more different between urban areas with industry and service industries as the economic pillars and counties with agriculture as the mainstay. Reconciling different needs and leveraging limited public resources is a challenge. There are two ideas for reform: First, strengthen the independence and autonomy of the county and weaken its connection with the urban area. The second is to expand the city and divide the county into districts.

The removal of counties and districts has expanded the urban area, integrated the local population, and transformed many farmers in the county into citizens, which is conducive to making full use of existing public services and giving play to the benefits of scale. Many cities that have withdrawn from counties and districts have also attracted more immigrants. These new populations have increased the size of the market and stimulated economic development. Dismantling counties and setting up districts also integrates land resources that are critical to urban development. With the merger of districts and counties, a large amount of rural land in suburban counties was converted into urban construction land, providing more space for economic development. However, in this process, many contradictions and conflicts have arisen due to the vastly different urban and rural land systems.

## 1.3 Complex information

Effective management requires critical information. However, information is complex and volatile, and the continuous collection and analysis of information requires a lot of resources at no small cost. Therefore, the party with an information advantage, or the party who can obtain information at a lower cost, naturally has a decision-making advantage.
